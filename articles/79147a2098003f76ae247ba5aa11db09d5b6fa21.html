Today, the Federal Trade Commission and five states settled their antitrust case against John Deere. The deal does something farmers have been demanding for more than a decade, forcing Deere to give farmers and independent repair shops the same repair software and diagnostic access it hands its authorized dealers. Huzzah!

Deere Has to Give Farmers Full Repair Capability

For the next ten years, under FTC and state supervision, Deere has to provide the stuff that actually gets a tractor running again in the field:

  • Reading, clearing, and resetting fault codes
  • Reprogramming electronic components and pairing new parts
  • Restarting equipment after an emissions-related “limp mode” shutdown
  • Full access to technical manuals, troubleshooting guides, and diagnostic information

And when Deere rolls out a new repair capability to more than half its dealers, it has to make that available to everyone too. Dealers are barred from punishing customers who choose to fix their own equipment.

“You bought the tractor. You should be able to fix it. It shouldn’t take a federal lawsuit to make that true,” said Kyle Wiens, iFixit CEO. “This is a huge win for farmers, who will finally get the tools and repair manuals they need to harvest their crops on time.”

Until now, a farmer with a dead tractor and a crop that needed to come out of the ground now had exactly one option: wait for a dealer technician, because Deere locked the software behind its own doors.

Over A Decade of Deere Trying to Protect a Repair Monopoly

We’ve been in this fight for over a decade. 

Back in 2015, iFixit launched the agricultural Right to Repair fight when we won farmers the legal right to get past the software locks on their own tractors. That exemption is what turned “you don’t really own your tractor” into a national movement. But it wasn’t enough: nobody who got around the software locks was legally allowed to share what they learned, and Deere’s tiered service software kept farmers from having the full capability to repair their own tractors. Eleven years later, the FTC is enforcing the exact principle we’ve been hammering on the whole time.

John Deere has been reluctant to share the information and tools that farmers need. The company has been flagrantly violating Colorado’s first-in-the-nation agricultural Right to Repair law. Deere has spent the time since that law went into effect in 2024 finding reasons not to fully live up to it. 

As Willie Cade of the Repair Association rightly puts it, “After years of fighting for the right to repair, this order gives farmers real hope. But promises on paper must become tools in farmers’ hands, and we will be watching implementation every step of the way.”

The next ten years are about whether Deere complies for real this time, and we intend to watch closely.

$1700 for a Manual Is Not Fair or Reasonable

Complying for real means ending evilly high prices for repair information. Charging farmers premium prices just to read the manual for a machine they already bought is indefensible. Deere demands $1,763.07 for the simple repair manual for the X9 combine. That’s extortion. It’s certainly not fair or reasonable, to quote the terms of today’s settlement. 

Repair manuals and the digital tools you need to fix your own equipment should come in the box with the machine. We hope Deere reads this settlement as the signal it is: the era of paywalling the instructions is over.

Farmers deserve the ability to fix their equipment. Now, hopefully, Deere will finally release the tools and manuals they need to get the job done.
