3 My Octopus Teacher

The unlikely undersea friendship that scored Oscar gold

With the barrage of new Netflix docs slowing a bit, I went back to one of my all-time favorites—the Oscar-winning delight that is 2020's My Octopus Teacher. The Best Documentary Feature winner (it also took the BAFTA, for good measure) remains one of Netflix's most beloved originals, and you should check it out if you haven't already.

The film follows South African filmmaker Craig Foster, burnt out and creatively adrift, who takes some time off and starts a daily regimen of diving in the underwater wonderland of the kelp forest near his home, close to the Cape of Good Hope. Foster narrates his near-spiritual experience of meeting and developing a bond with a young, curious octopus. Over the course of a year, Foster interacts with her, watches her hunt, outsmart sharks, and recover from a near-fatal attack that cost her an arm.

Directors Pippa Ehrlich and James Reed build the doc around a candid sit-down interview with Foster, interspersed with some gorgeous underwater footage, as the story shifts into one about Foster's own recovery and about fatherhood.

2 Obsessed

Snack-sized profiles of some of the world's neatest pastimes

I have been absolutely enjoying the fruits of Netflix's new content deals with publishers such as Condé Nast, America's Test Kitchen, and more, which have been offering all kinds of short-form shows that you can find on the Netflix home page, as of August 3. I've been including them in my last few Netflix docs roundups, and this weekend I've found another one—Wired's long-running series Obsessed, an incredible collection of highly bingeable, compact profiles of people who've taken their unique hobbies to extremes.

Twenty-two fascinating "Greatest Hits" shorts, all between seven and ten minutes each, are collected for you to binge, giving you a snapshot into the lives of some one-of-a-kind people, including Aaron Yoder, the world's fastest backwards runner; Canadian Lego artist Ekow Nimako, who creates incredible works out of black bricks only; 3D pavement artist Kurt Wenner and his vertigo-inducing optical illusion drawings; and the delightful Joanna Fang, a Foley artist at PlayStation Studios who's having the best time smashing, crinkling, and stomping things to make sounds for video games like God of War: Ragnarök.

The episodes blend short interviews with the subjects, mixed with footage and demonstrations of their hobbies and work. Obsessed is the literal definition of bingeable programming. You don't have to watch them in any order, and you can crush a bunch for a cool fascination-infusion.

1 Freefall: A Reckoning for Boeing

The Boeing mess shifts focus to the death of a whistleblower

If you're a frequent flyer who actually pays attention to the make and model of the aircraft you're on, then you may be watching this through your fingers. My top pick this weekend just, er, landed on Netflix. Freefall: A Reckoning for Boeing is the sequel to director Rory Kennedy's 2022 Netflix doc Downfall: The Case Against Boeing (also streaming), but where Downfall's focus was on two fatal 737 Max crashes in 2018 and 2019, Freefall shifts to the fallout that followed, and the 2024 suicide of whistleblower John Barnett, a veteran Boeing quality control manager.

The enlightening feature-length documentary opens with footage from Barnett's video deposition, and his lawyers detailing how he was found dead in his truck in a hotel parking lot after only the second day. The film moves forward, tracing Barnett's career, then widening its scope as other insiders join the conversation in candid, revealing interviews—including high-profile whistleblower Santiago Paredes, a quality control inspector at Boeing fuselage supplier Spirit AeroSystems, nicknamed "Showstopper" for holding up deliveries over defects.

Built largely on those interviews and archival news footage, the mind-boggling 93-minute, R-rated movie argues that Boeing's cost-cutting culture and cover-ups led to safety failures, putting passengers at risk for years—and that nothing has really changed.
