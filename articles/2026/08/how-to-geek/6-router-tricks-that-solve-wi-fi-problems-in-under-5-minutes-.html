Move your router somewhere better

It could be sitting in the exact wrong spot

Router placement is more important than it seems, and your Wi-Fi might be dealing with dead zones, where the signal isn't as strong. Many of us stuff the router in whatever place is convenient and fairly hidden, because hey, routers aren't exactly decorative ... but that doesn't help their performance whatsoever.

Aim for something central, elevated, and out in the open. Think a shelf and not the floor, but also a shelf and not a closed cabinet.

Check the signal strength on your phone in the worst room and see whether moving the router helps at all. It very well might, or might I even say, it should.

Pick the right band

Don't let your router do this for you

When picking between 2.4GHz and 5GHz (or maybe even 6GHz), you might be tempted to always go for the highest number. I don't blame you, but that's not how this thing works.

2.4GHz travels furthest and gets through walls best, so if you shove all of your stuff onto the 6GHz band, some devices are going to have a bad time. The ideal setup is to match the device to the band and not let your router do any guesswork whatsoever. In fact, doing this helped me fix some Wi-Fi issues I've had myself.

Worth noting that you should never disable the 2.4GHz band entirely. Just let your less demanding devices cruise along on the longer-range 2.4GHz band, and move the ones that need speed to 5GHz.

Narrow your 2.4GHz channel width

Wider isn't faster

Here's another setting where letting your router handle all the decision making can end badly. If it's set to auto or 20/40MHz on the 2.4GHz band, you may feel like setting it up at a straight 20MHz is counterintuitive, but it can genuinely help if your Wi-Fi is acting up.

It's true that wider channels carry more data, but the entire 2.4GHz band is only a bit over 83MHz wide, so if you bond two channels together, the majority of it will be gone in one go.

The payoff to sticking to 20MHz is stability, which is what you're looking for here. And, as I said above, most of the stuff that uses 2.4GHz isn't going to demand a monster-like connection, so you'll win stability without sacrificing anything much.

Split your bands into two networks

Smart Connect isn't always the smartest way to go

Many routers, especially those provided by your ISP, ship with all the bands bundled under one network name, plus a feature called Smart Connect (or band steering). That feature decides which band houses which device. As mentioned, your router makes the wrong decisions with things like that, so it's better to take the time to set it right. Band steering tends to prioritize 5GHz; an unnecessary decision that may lower the quality of your Wi-Fi.

The cleanest way to deal with this debacle is, in my opinion, to split these two bands (2.4GHz and 5GHz) into two separate SSIDs. It'll take about two minutes in your router settings and it'll make setting up all the devices much easier. Once you decide what goes where based on use case and distance, you might see improvements in your Wi-Fi.

Turn off DFS channels for a few days

A quick fix with a longer testing time

This is a fix for something else entirely. Well, kind of. If your 5GHz network occasionally vanishes for a minute or two and then comes back, don't blame your router right away—there could be more to it.

Parts of the 5GHz band are shared with radar systems, and any router sitting on one of those channels is legally required to stop transmitting and move elsewhere when it detects a radar signal. This whole mechanism is called Dynamic Frequency Selection.

Some of you are probably frowning right now, having never heard of any such things. Some of us are more exposed to this than others; those who live near airports, coastlines, or weather radar installations, may have this happen more than others.

In any case, to fix this, find the DFS toggle in your 5GHz settings or manually pin the router to channels 36 to 48 or 149 to 165. Those sit outside of radar ranges.

What does DHCP stand for, and what is its main function on a home network?

Perfect! DHCP (Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol) is the unsung hero of home networking. Every time a device joins your network, your router's DHCP server automatically hands it a unique IP address, subnet mask, and gateway info so it can communicate without manual configuration.
DHCP stands for Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol, and its job is to automatically assign IP addresses to devices on your network. Without it, you'd have to manually configure a unique IP address on every single phone, laptop, and smart device — a tedious nightmare!

QoS — Quality of Service — is actually about traffic prioritization. By tagging certain data types (like VoIP calls or gaming packets) as high priority, your router ensures latency-sensitive applications get bandwidth first, even when the network is congested.

The WAN (Wide Area Network) port connects your router to your ISP's modem or gateway — essentially your entry point to the internet. The LAN (Local Area Network) ports are for connecting devices inside your home. Mixing them up can cause your network to not function at all!
