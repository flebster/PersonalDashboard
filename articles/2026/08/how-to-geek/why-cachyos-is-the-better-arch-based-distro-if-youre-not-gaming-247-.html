Try something different:

CachyOS has earned a reputation for being fast, but because speed is easiest to benchmark in frame rates, it’s often discussed in the context of gaming too.

However, CachyOS's real advantage shows up in other applications and workflows. If your machine spends its day compiling code, editing video, exporting files, or running a dozen heavy applications at once, CachyOS's advantages apply to you more directly than they do to someone trying to squeeze another 8 FPS out of a game.

CachyOS takes Arch in a different direction

A new way to get packages

At its core, CachyOS is just Arch Linux. It has all the same packages, AUR, and the same "bleeding edge" access that makes Arch what it is. The most significant difference is how you handle downloading and installing those assets.

CachyOS rebuilds most (if not all) of its packages specifically for more recent CPUs (v3 and v4 builds), trading backward compatibility for older hardware in exchange for optimized performance on modern processors. That gives you a performance edge in many common tasks a work PC might need to perform.

You also get a customizable kernel specifically designed to give you a responsive desktop experience. Critically, you don't have to break out a textbook and read 200 wiki pages to do that. CachyOS provides a pre-configured environment with snapshots and drivers available as soon as you boot.

Arch has Arch Installer, which is a big improvement, but CachyOS is still more friendly.

Less assembly required

CachyOS's "usable out of the box" approach is an improvement over other versions of Arch. Normally, getting Arch running and learning to maintain it is a pretty significant hurdle.

CachyOS handles much of the tedium—partitioning, working with the bootloader, and driver installation (including NVIDIA)—and lets you choose your desktop environment without building everything from scratch.

Additionally, it automatically creates snapshots during installation and keeps a restore point. If something goes wrong while you're running an update, you can just roll back.

CachyOS isn't purpose-built for gaming

It is a capable gaming distro, but that isn't its main focus

With the ability to customize your setup to maximize performance, it is natural to think that CachyOS would be a great gaming distro. In many ways, it is. You can squeeze some extra performance out of games, and the handheld edition is reportedly quite good.

CachyOS exists to provide a more approachable, intuitive way to get a general-purpose Arch distro up and running. Processing photos, compiling and testing code, or running virtual machines are all processor-heavy tasks that come up all the time if you work on your PC. Those sorts of tasks are exactly what CachyOS's rebuilt packages improve. If you look at the project's recent releases, you'll notice the focus on Python performance and math library fixes—the kind of thing that improves general or work performance rather than gaming performance specifically.

Gaming performance often depends more on proprietary GPU drivers (if you use NVIDIA), which aren't the kind of things that CachyOS can just rebuild and eke out a single-digit performance boost.

You don't get a game recompiled

CachyOS's performance boosts come from its ability to match your software to your PC's hardware. In effect, you get to ditch the bloat and compatibility components you don't need.

However, you can't apply the same trick to games (or at least most of them). You're stuck with pre-compiled binaries that are designed to work on as many systems as possible. If you're lucky, it was built with native Linux gaming in mind. Otherwise, you'll need to lean on a compatibility layer like Proton, which has a marginal overhead that can slightly impact performance.

That doesn't make CachyOS bad at gaming, but that means that CachyOS's central trick—rebuilding software for modern hardware—just isn't possible for games. The best you can do is optimize everything that supports game performance instead. And, since Linux is less bloated than Windows in general, you can often get better performance on CachyOS than you would on Windows, even if the games aren't optimized or designed to run natively on Linux.

CachyOS beyond gaming

When you're talking about a PC that you use day in and day out, either for work or personally, convenience and reliability are critical. The most "cutting edge" packages don't matter one bit if you spend more time fighting with your PC than actually using it.

Nothing that makes CachyOS good is specific to gaming. Rebuilt packages and a snappy kernel help a compile job or a video export just as much as they help with frame rate. In other words, the benefits to gaming are generally incidental. Still, CachyOs is a great fit for developers or editors who want current software and don't mind a bit of manual upkeep to get the most out of their existing hardware.

The only potential problem is that "bleeding edge" occasionally means "a bit unstable."
