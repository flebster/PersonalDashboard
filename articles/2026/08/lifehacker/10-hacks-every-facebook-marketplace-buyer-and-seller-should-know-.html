10 Hacks Every Facebook Marketplace Buyer and Seller Should Know

How to get the most out of the algorithm, avoid the hassles, and score major bargains.

Through its focus on local selling, Facebook Marketplace has become the world's biggest digital garage sale, but without tables to browse and friendly small talk. Getting what you want, whether it's finding those low-priced gems or getting a good price for your old blender, comes down to understanding how both the platform and its users work. You can't treat it like an old-school classifieds page and hope for the best; you need tactics. Here are 10 strategic hacks to navigate the chaos, outsmart the feed, and get the best deals on both sides of the transaction.

Download Google Lens, the most important app for both buyers and sellers

Whether you're a buyer or a seller, you should download and use Google Lens. This scans a photograph, identifies the item pictured, and spits out all kinds of info about it, including prices from new and second-hand sales. It's an instant price-check that lets sellers know if their asking amount is in line with other sales, and it lets sellers know whether they're getting a good deal or not.

Here's a short step-by-step guide for using Google Lens on mobile:

  • Tap the Marketplace listing photo to open it full screen.

  • Take a screenshot or save the photo to your camera roll.

  • Open the Google app on your phone and tap the Camera/Lens icon located on the right side of the search bar.

  • Select your screenshot from your photos.

  • Lens will scan the item and pop up the exact product name, reviews, online retail pages, eBay sold listings, and more, so you know its real value before making an offer.

Other apps that might be useful for sellers include Facebook's own Seller, an inventory management tool that organizes your listings and fully separates your Marketplace activity from your Facebook activity, and Crosslist, an app that lets you create one ad that can be posted on 11 different sites, from eBay to Poshmark.

Change your location on Facebook Marketplace to find items for sale in more desirable areas

Marketplace's focus on location-based sales makes it a mosaic of micro-economies instead of a unified market. Supply, demand, and seller desperation fluctuate wildly based on demographic and geographical lines, so expand your search beyond your zip code and target micro-markets. Here are some ideas:

  • Wealthy suburbs: If you want high-end furniture, power tools, and designer clothing, target sellers in affluent neighborhoods who might value cleaning out their garage more than wringing every dollar out of a listing.

  • College towns: The area around a university at the end of a semester can be a goldmine if you're looking for standing desks, computer monitors, and other student-y items. Kids with a hard move-out date and a plane ticket home will often sell their mini-fridges for almost nothing.

  • City centers: Apartment dwellers moving between small spaces face strict lease dates and lack storage space, so urban cores are prime hunting grounds for lower-priced furniture, kitchen gadgets, and TV mounts.

How to set up a targeted search:

  1. On desktop or mobile, click the Location filter at the top of your Marketplace feed (e.g., "Los Angeles, CA · Within 40 mi").

  2. Enter the specific ZIP code or town name of your target micro-market, rather than your home address.

  3. Shrink the radius slider down to 5 or 10 miles to isolate listings exclusively within that neighborhood.

Include one honest flaw in your Marketplace product descriptions

It seems almost counterintuitive, but when you're writing a product description, mentioning a flaw with a product you're selling on Marketplace can actually make it more desirable to buyers than saying it's pristine. Everyone is looking for an honest seller, and mentioning a missing knob on a stereo or a small scratch on a bookcase proves you're not trying to hide anything, and it makes people more prone to believe the good things you say about the product. It's a reassuring way of answering the unspoken question everyone has when buying: "What's wrong with it?"

Game the Facebook Marketplace algorithm with a simple video

I'm shocked by how many people don't do this, but if you're a seller, you should include a video with every listing, even for things like sunglasses. First, because Marketplace's algorithm will place items with videos into more people's feeds. Secondly, it's "proof of life" and a reassurance that you're legit. Film the drawers sliding smoothly, the blender motor running, or a quick 360-degree walkaround in daylight and you've proven that the item exists, works, and matches your photos. Instant legitimacy.

Use the "list in more places" feature to target niche groups

After you post a listing, tap the three dots on it and select "List in more places." Facebook will surface relevant Buy/Sell groups based on your item and location. A lot of sellers skip this step, but listing in these groups connects you with more people and a more informed community than a general ad. It doesn't cost anything, and it doesn't take long, so add your listing to every suggested group that's a reasonable fit.

One thing to watch, though: Match your tone to the group. A post that reads fine as a standalone Marketplace listing can feel generic or spammy when dropped into a tight-knit local group.

Know when to "re-list" vs. "renew" an ad

If the ad you've posted doesn't sell and the views have slowed to a trickle, you have two options: renew or re-list. To deal with dead ads, Marketplace gives sellers a "renew" option for ads that have been up for seven days. The specifics of Marketplace's algorithm are secret, but insiders say that renewing only gives a brief, small boost to a listing. Deleting the ad entirely and putting up an identical one makes the item new again, but it should be done with caution. Facebook doesn't seem to like when users remove an item and replace it too often; it can even result in having your account flagged for suspicious activity. It also results in anyone looking at your profile seeing the same item listed and delisted, which is a red flag to many buyers. So you have to be patient. According to insiders, if you take down an ad and wait two months to re-list, it should show up as clean, but I can't say for sure, because the algorithm is mysterious.

Follow this simple rule for Marketplace price negotiations

I can't stand haggling, but I've grown to accept that it must be done on Marketplace. A seller who states their price is firm will still be low-balled, and a buyer paying the sticker price is usually paying too much. You can get deep into the weeds when it comes to price negotiations, but for buying a second-hand sofa or something, it's easier to follow the 15% rule: If you're selling, tack on an extra 15% to be haggled away. If you're buying, offer 15% less than the listed price.

Do this to be the first one to see new listings of items you really want

This hack isn't for one-timers or occasional buyers and sellers, but if you're a hobbyist or collector, it's gold: When you've successfully purchased a Pokémon card or something from a seller you vibe with, give them your number and a friendly, "If you're selling any more Pikachus, send me a text. I'm always in the market." This can also work for parents of young kids who are buying hand-me-down clothing, toys, or electronics from other parents with older kids...those kids are going to keep outgrowing this year's jeans, and you can be the first one to put dibs on them.

Dealing with a solid buyer saves sellers a lot of time weeding out flakes and low-ballers. As a buyer, getting a first look at coveted items gives you an advantage over the competition. And for sellers, it's easier dealing with someone you've already done business with before (and know they're going to show up to get the item when they say they will).

Filter for $1 to find potential steals

If you're new to Marketplace, you may be confused by the number of items listed for $1. People generally list for $1 for two reasons: to game the "sort by lowest price" algorithm to get their ad to the top of searches, or because they're selling multiple items with one ad. This is generally a bad strategy for sellers but can be great for buyers. I ignore the "gaming search results" group entirely—it feels dishonest, and the lack of a listed price gives you no starting point for a negotiation—but people listing a lot of things in one ad can lead to some great deals. Whether it's an estate sale, a yard sale, or a recording studio shutting down, some sellers post a single wide shot of a room full of stuff, set the price to $1, and spell out the specific contents and individual prices in the ad's description. Taking the time to click on these lot listings and reading through the inventory cuts out a ton of competition from would-be buyers who only search by keywords.

Use the two-hour test window to hack the algorithm

Always be around to answer questions for the first couple hours after you make a post on Marketplace. Marketplace shows new listings to a small test audience to see how many people click, save, or message, so the algorithm can determine how widely to push it. A lot more goes into how widely your ad is flung (price, desirability, etc.), but if you're there right after posting to provide engagement, it helps your ad be seen by a wider audience later on.

Speaking of timing, there's a variety of opinions on when is the best time to post a listing, but there's no consensus. Some swear by 6–9 p.m., especially Thursday through Sunday, when people are off work and scrolling from the couch. Others point to weekday mornings, when there's a burst of activity as people ease into the workday.

What you'll find for sale on Facebook Marketplace

No hack in the world is going to help you sell something no one wants to buy, so I've put together a general hierarchy of what sells on Marketplace and what doesn't, in order of cheapest to most expensive.

Furniture and heirlooms at bargain prices

The greatest bargains on Marketplace are in heavy things with specific purposes. Above-ground pools, hot-tubs, pianos, and heavy china cabinets sell for fractions of their original price, and they're often marked down to "haul it away and you can have it" prices. Some unwanted items are amazing, too. A flagship console organ with double keyboards and foot pedals might have retailed for $20,000 in the 70s, but you can routinely find these 300-pound behemoths listed for $0 on Marketplace. If I had a barn, I'd get like seven of them and throw an amazing party.

Here are other things sellers will struggle with but buyers can have for a song:

  • Outdated, general interest technology: 720p LCD TVs, old wifi routers, and non-4K computer monitors are tough sales. New technology is just too inexpensive for many to want to step backwards.

  • Particle board furniture: IKEA flatpack bookcases and couches aren't really meant to be transported and sold.

  • Formal heirlooms: To our parents and grandparents, fine china, crystal stemware, silver-plated flatware, and porcelain figurines like Precious Moments or Hummel seemed like investments in the future, but it turns out the future doesn't host too many formal dinner parties, and your parents and grandparents probably didn't have good enough taste to collect rare, mid-century patterns that remain desirable.

Hobby and sports items at low prices

The next tier is the junkyard of failed hobbies, where sellers unload their old marching band saxophones, hand-me-down skateboards, entry-level bicycles, and rowing machines for "I just want this out of my garage" prices. So many bargains to be found for starting a new hobby.

Mid-tier products for users who are trading up to the next level

There are some categories that have a huge middle ground. Things like bicycles, audio equipment, and camera gear tend to have a large, dedicated core of users always trading up, so there's a lot of churn in the middle. Sellers tend to know what these things are worth, so you're more likely to find modest deals, but they're definitely cheaper than new.

What you're unlikely to find: high-end, specialized gear

High-end, specialized gear—commercial embroidery machines, boutique espresso set-ups, high-end woodworking machinery, etc—is rarely sold on Marketplace. These are often "keep until you're gone" items. Sellers tend to use non-local platforms to sell these, as the buyers are niche and know exactly what they want and what they want to pay.
