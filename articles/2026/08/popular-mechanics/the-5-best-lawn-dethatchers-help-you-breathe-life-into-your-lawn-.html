    • A layer of dead organic material on your lawn, called thatch, is great in moderation. A thin layer of thatch acts as a buffer that keeps the soil moist, regulates its temperature, and adds nutrients as it breaks down. But if that thatch gets too thick, it can prevent water and oxygen from reaching the soil, choking your grass.

    One way to keep thatch in check is to remove it periodically using a dethatcher. This yard tool uses sharp metal tines to pull thatch up off your lawn, so fertilizer, air, and water can reach your grass’s roots. Dethatchers come in a variety of types, including specialized dethatching rakes, electric dethatchers that resemble walk-behind lawn mowers, and tow-behind models that attach to the back of a lawn tractor or zero-turn mower.

    If you want a lush green lawn that’s the envy of your neighbors, you need a dethatcher in your arsenal of lawn care tools. We’ve tested and researched a variety of different dethatchers to recommend those best suited to handle yards of every size.

    What to Consider

    Type

    Dethatchers come in three different types. Dethatching rakes are suitable for small yards, while electric walk-behind dethatchers can handle larger yards. If you own a lawn tractor and have a lot of ground to cover, then a tow-behind model probably makes the most sense.

    Like a standard rake, a dethatching rake has a long wooden or composite handle that attaches to a set of tines. The head is typically 12 to 17 inches wide with sharp curved tines that bite into thatch, so you can pull it up. Unlike a standard rake, a dethatching rake's head is adjustable, allowing you to adjust the angle to set how aggressively it bites into the soil. Along with dethatching, you can also use a dethatching rake to lightly till soil in a garden to prep it for planting. A manual dethatching rake is the most affordable option for dethatching; however, it’s also the most labor intensive. Be ready for an upper body workout when using a dethatching rake.

    Electric dethatchers look similar to walk-behind electric lawn mowers. But rather than a horizontal spinning blade, they have a cylinder with rows of tines. A motor spins the cylinder, which digs into turf to pull up thatch as you push it. These have adjustable heights, so you can regulate how much thatch you remove with each pass. Electric dethatchers make the job of dethatching less labor intensive than a dethatching rake, but they cost more.

    Electric dethatchers come in corded and cordless models. While corded models offer unlimited runtime, they’re restricted by the length of an extension cord. Cordless models are more convenient to use, but they’re pricier and limited to a runtime of around 45 minutes before they need a recharge.

    A tow behind dethatcher consists of a wide bar with a row of tines that sits on two wheels and has a tow hitch that attaches to the back of a riding lawn mower. Tow behind dethatchers are 4 to 5 feet wide, so you can cover a much larger area than you can with a rake or walkbehind motorized dethatcher. If you have a lawn tractor and a yard that spans half an acre or more, you’ll want a tow behind dethatcher.

    Width

    Dethatchers vary in width depending on type. Dethatching rakes range from 12 inches to 17 inches wide. While a wider rake can cover more ground with each pass, it requires more force to pull up thatch. Walk-behind thatchers are around 14 to 15 inches wide, while tow-behind dethatchers range from about 4 to 5 feet wide. The wider the dethatcher, the more thatch it can remove with each pass.

    Dethatching and Scarifying

    Many manual and electric dethatchers can scarify as well as dethatch. Dethatching uses flexible tines that rake over the soil to remove clumps of thatch, while scarifying uses thin blades that cut deeply into the soil to remove moss and thicker layers of thatch. You can scarify with a rake by holding it at a more aggressive angle. Walk-behind dethatchers have interchangeable heads for scarifying and dethatching. You can scarify with a tow-behind dethatcher by lowering its tines and adding weight to the machine’s tray.

    Scarifying removes moss and thicker layers of thatch. You can also use a scarifier to break up the soil to prep it for overseeding. Only scarify when necessary, as this process stresses your lawn more than dethatching.

    FAQs

    How frequently you dethatch your lawn really depends on the amount of thatch build up you get. For high thatch build up, you should dethatch once annually. For less severe thatch issues, once every two or three years should suffice.

    If you’re not sure, check to see if the thatch layer is more than a 1/2-inch thick. How often you dethatch can also depend on the type of lawn you have. Warm season grasses like Bermuda, Zoysia. And St. Augustine tends to build up thatch faster than cool season grasses.

    Detaching stresses your lawn, so only do it during peak growing seasons. By dethatching in the spring or early fall, your lawn will recover more quickly than during dormant seasons in winter and summer.

    While a dethatcher is the best way to remove thatch when it becomes too thick, there are measures you can take to prevent it from building up. Avoid over fertilizing your lawn, which can cause thatch to accumulate. Water deeply twice a week, as frequent shallow watering can cause roots to come to the surface, where they die and create thatch. When you mow, only remove a third of the grass height to prevent clippings from building up on the lawn. Finally, aerate your lawn once annually to improve air and water penetration that helps to break down thatch.

    Start by getting it off the grass. Dead thatch sitting on top of your lawn will block sunlight and air, hurting your grass and negating the reason you dethatched in the first place. Rake the thatch into a pile and then collect it in a bag, or use a lawn mower with a bagger attachment to mow it up. Some machines, like the Wen and SunJoe listed above, come with an onboard collection bag, but you may still want to pass over the yard with a rake or mower to be sure you get all the material up.

    Once you’ve collected the thatch, the best option is to compost it. You can also use thatch as garden mulch as it will help improve soil quality as it breaks down. If you’ve treated your lawn with fertilizer or pest control, don’t use the thatch in a vegetable garden.
